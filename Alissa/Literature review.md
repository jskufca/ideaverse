# A Model Literature Review
## Modeling the Effects of Urban Green Spaces on Crime

*Prepared as a reference example for revising Chapter 2 of A. Whiteley's dissertation*

This review is organized around the dissertation's three guiding questions — (1) does greening reduce crime enticement in surrounding areas, (2) is there a minimum effective size for a greened area, and (3) does distributing a fixed greening budget across multiple small sites change its impact — and is structured in three parts. Part 1 covers the environmental-criminology theory that explains *why* altering the physical and social environment of a block should change crime patterns at all. Part 2 covers the empirical record on greening and vacant-lot remediation specifically. Part 3 covers the mathematical and computational modeling literature that the dissertation's own ODE and agent-based models extend. A closing synthesis ties these threads to the specific gap the dissertation fills.

---

## 1. Theoretical Foundations in Environmental Criminology

### 1.1 Routine activity theory and the crime triangle

Cohen and Felson's routine activity theory reframed crime not as a product of offender pathology alone but as the convergence, in time and space, of a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian (Cohen & Felson, 1979). This "crime triangle" is useful for a modeling dissertation precisely because it is structural: it identifies the levers an intervention can pull. Greening a vacant lot can remove a *target/location* (a place to stash contraband or loiter unseen), and, by drawing people into the space for recreation, can add *guardians* whose mere presence disrupts the convergence the theory describes. Routine activity theory also explains why crime clusters around offenders' everyday paths (home, work, and the routes between), which is the theoretical bridge into near-repeat victimization below and directly motivates treating enticement as a spatial field rather than a per-incident probability.

### 1.2 Broken windows, incivilities, and their limits

Wilson and Kelling's broken windows thesis argues that visible physical disorder — litter, graffiti, untended lots — signals an absence of social control and thereby invites further, more serious offending (Wilson & Kelling, 1982). Keizer, Lindenberg, and Steg (2008) supplied some of the most-cited experimental support: across six field studies in the Netherlands, visible disorder (graffiti, litter, a broken fence) roughly doubled the rate at which bystanders committed a second, unrelated norm violation such as littering or minor theft, consistent with disorder spreading rather than staying contained. This is the mechanism the dissertation's model encodes as the "dynamic enticement" term that rises with local crime and diffuses to neighbors.

It is important that a dissertation resting on the broken-windows mechanism also engage its most serious critique. Harcourt (2001) reanalyzed the New York City data most often cited in favor of the theory and argued that the statistical association between disorder and serious crime disappears once poverty, residential instability, and race are controlled for, and that much of the "success" attributed to order-maintenance policing instead reflects the sheer scale of misdemeanor arrests. Citing Harcourt does not require abandoning the modeling framework — enticement/attractiveness dynamics are a reasonable formal analogue regardless of whether "disorder causes crime" in the strong causal sense Wilson and Kelling proposed — but a rigorous literature review should acknowledge that the theory is contested rather than presenting it as settled fact.

### 1.3 Defensible space and crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED)

A second design tradition, largely absent from the dissertation's current review, explains greening's effect through *guardianship and surveillance* rather than disorder per se. Jacobs (1961) argued in *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* that mixed-use, well-trafficked streets are safe streets because they generate "eyes on the street" — ordinary people incidentally watching public space as a byproduct of using it. Jeffery (1971) formalized this into crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED), arguing that the built environment could be deliberately shaped to reduce criminal opportunity. Newman (1972) operationalized these ideas empirically in *Defensible Space*, showing from New York City public-housing data that buildings and grounds designed to give residents clear sightlines, defined territory, and a sense of ownership had markedly lower crime than otherwise-similar developments lacking these features.

This tradition matters for the dissertation's modeling choices in a very direct way: it supplies the mechanism by which a *park*, and not merely an *empty cleared lot*, breaks the crime triangle. A cleared lot removes a hiding place; a park that draws in daily foot traffic (natural surveillance) and a maintaining community (territoriality) adds guardians. Lin, Wang, and Huang (2021) extend this logic specifically to street trees and greenery, finding that vegetation characteristics that preserve sightlines (e.g., pruned canopy, low ground cover) are associated with crime reductions, whereas dense, sightline-blocking vegetation is not — a moderating condition the dissertation's uniform "greened zone" abstraction does not yet capture but should acknowledge as a modeling simplification.

### 1.4 Social disorganization and collective efficacy

Sampson, Raudenbush, and Earls (1997) offered an influential neighborhood-level account that complicates a purely physical-design story: in a multilevel study of Chicago neighborhoods, they found that "collective efficacy" — social cohesion among residents combined with their shared willingness to intervene on behalf of the common good — was a stronger predictor of reduced violence than concentrated poverty or residential instability considered alone. This theory is the natural counterpart to the physical-design literature above and offers a plausible explanation for the dissertation's own finding (Section 1.2 of the dissertation) that varying socioeconomic conditions matter to greening's effectiveness: a greened lot in a neighborhood with high collective efficacy may activate guardianship far more readily than an identical lot where social ties are weak. This suggests collective efficacy, or a proxy for it, is a natural candidate parameter for future extensions of the model.

### 1.5 Repeat and near-repeat victimization

Repeat victimization research shows that crime is highly concentrated on a small set of repeat targets: using British Crime Survey and Stockholm victimization data, Pease and Laycock (1996) reported that roughly 44% of recorded crime was committed against only 4% of victims. Johnson, Bowers, and Hirschfield (1997) extended this spatially, showing that burglary risk elevates not only at the victimized address but at nearby addresses for a period afterward — the "near-repeat" effect. Townsley, Homel, and Chaseling (2003) tested this hypothesis directly against Australian burglary data and confirmed a significant space-time clustering effect that decayed over roughly two months, with the effect stronger in neighborhoods of homogeneous housing stock (where a successful method of entry, or knowledge gained from one burglary, transfers readily to a similar neighboring property). Together, these studies motivate exactly the diffusion term the dissertation borrows from Short et al. (2008): near-repeat victimization is the empirical signature that a formal model of *spatially diffusing* enticement is meant to reproduce.

## 2. Empirical Evidence on Greening, Vacant Land, and Crime

### 2.1 What greening interventions look like in practice

"Greening" spans a range of interventions — trash and debris removal, land grading, planting grass and trees, and installing low perimeter fencing, followed by ongoing maintenance — most of which originated in city-scale blight-remediation programs. Philadelphia's LandCare program, run by the Pennsylvania Horticultural Society since 1999 in response to roughly 30,000 lots left vacant by mid-century deindustrialization, is the source of most of the field's strongest evidence (Branas et al., 2011; Branas et al., 2018). The federal Weed and Seed program (U.S. Department of Justice, Community Capacity Development Office, 2004) paired law-enforcement "weeding" with community-development "seeding" and is a useful point of contrast, since it combines greening-adjacent investment with active policing rather than isolating the design intervention itself.

### 2.2 Foundational trials in Philadelphia

Branas and colleagues (2011) conducted a decade-long difference-in-differences analysis comparing treated and matched control vacant lots in Philadelphia and found that greening was associated with statistically significant, citywide reductions in gun assaults and, in some sections of the city, reductions in vandalism, alongside residents reporting less stress and more outdoor exercise. Branas et al. (2018) followed with a citywide cluster-randomized trial — one of the strongest causal designs in this literature — randomizing 541 vacant lots to greening, trash cleanup, or no intervention. They again found reduced gun assaults and reduced fear of crime among nearby residents, with no evidence that crime was simply displaced to adjacent blocks, and noted (as a genuine anomaly worth flagging in any review) a small increase in reported disorderly conduct near greened lots. Garvin, Cannuscio, and Branas (2013) ran an earlier, smaller randomized controlled trial using the same greening protocol and likewise found reduced gun assaults, with residents reporting significantly greater feelings of safety — useful as a robustness check that the 2018 citywide results were not an artifact of scale.

### 2.3 Moderating conditions: land use context

Macdonald and colleagues (2021) asked a question the dissertation's own model is built to explore computationally: does the surrounding context change how much a greened lot helps? Using the same Philadelphia greening protocol, they found that lots near "crime generators" — specifically transit stations and bars — saw a smaller crime-reduction benefit than lots in more residential settings, though the overall citywide effect remained a reduction. Testing for displacement using successively larger buffer rings around each lot, they found no significant increase in nearby crime and some evidence that immediately adjacent buffer areas experienced a modest additional reduction — an empirical analogue to the "buffer zone" effect the dissertation's own agent-based simulations reproduce.

### 2.4 Effects beyond Philadelphia: Flint and Youngstown

The Philadelphia results generalize, with caveats, to other legacy cities. Pizarro and colleagues (2020) studied Flint, Michigan's Clean and Green program (2005–2014) and found that increased greening, and especially active maintenance (mowed grass), was associated with the emergence of localized crime "cold spots," and that community perceptions of safety improved. Kondo, Hohl, Han, and Branas (2016) studied Youngstown, Ohio's "Lots of Green" program and distinguished two treatment types — a stabilization treatment (cleanup and greening) and a community-reuse treatment (largely community gardens) — finding statistically significant reductions across multiple crime categories for at least one treatment type, with the largest effects for felony assault, alongside a partially offsetting increase in motor vehicle theft that is worth noting as a genuine contradiction rather than smoothing it over.

### 2.5 Psychosocial outcomes: fear, mental health, and community engagement

Because enticement in the dissertation's model is explicitly meant to capture perceived, not just recorded, disorder, the psychosocial literature is directly relevant. South, Hohl, Kondo, MacDonald, and Branas (2018) used the same Philadelphia cluster-randomized trial as Branas et al. (2018) to test mental-health outcomes and found that greening produced a significant reduction in self-reported depression and worthlessness, with the largest effects among residents below the poverty line — evidence that greening's benefits are not confined to crime counts and may operate partly through the community's psychological relationship to its environment. Burt, Kondo, and colleagues (2022) similarly linked community greening to both reduced fear of crime and improved mental-health outcomes, while Gong and colleagues (2023) tested a "Busy Streets" hypothesis — that greening's crime-reduction benefit is amplified when paired with active community engagement in maintaining the space — and found support for community engagement as a moderating, not merely additive, factor.

### 2.6 Boundary conditions and contradictory findings

A responsible literature review must also report where the evidence disagrees or where mechanisms are known to break down, rather than leaving this as an unfinished placeholder. Morgan (2001), studying repeat burglary in a Perth suburb, found that burglary risk did not map cleanly onto neighborhood income: some low-income neighborhoods showed elevated repeat-burglary risk consistent with a "high crime, low guardianship" story, but some higher-socioeconomic-status neighborhoods showed comparably high burglary rates, driven instead by target attractiveness (higher-value goods) rather than an absence of guardianship. Lin, Wang, and Huang's (2021) finding that vegetation type moderates whether trees help or hurt sightlines is a second, environment-specific boundary condition. Together these findings argue against treating "greening reduces enticement" as a universal law and toward the dissertation's own stated position that neighborhood-specific factors must be considered — a position that is better supported by naming the specific studies that motivate it (as above) than by the general, uncited assertion in the current draft.

## 3. Mathematical and Computational Models of Crime

### 3.1 The statistical (agent-based) model of criminal behavior

Short, D'Orsogna, Pasour, Tita, Brantingham, Bertozzi, and Chayes (2008) is the foundational model the dissertation directly extends. In their agent-based framework, each site on a lattice carries an "attractiveness" (what the dissertation calls enticement) composed of a static baseline and a dynamic component that rises with local criminal activity, decays over time, and diffuses to neighboring sites; criminals move preferentially toward higher-attractiveness sites and commit crimes probabilistically as a function of local attractiveness. The model was constructed specifically to reproduce the broken-windows and near-repeat phenomena described in Sections 1.2 and 1.5 above from first principles, and its central achievement was showing that spatial hotspots of crime can emerge endogenously from purely local interaction rules, without any exogenous hotspot being assumed. The dissertation's own dynamic-enticement equations are a direct descendant of this framework.

### 3.2 Reaction-diffusion and PDE hotspot models

Short, Bertozzi, and Brantingham (2010) took the discrete lattice model above to its continuum limit, deriving a system of reaction-diffusion partial differential equations for attractiveness and criminal density and studying how crime hotspots form, persist, and — critically for a dissertation about *intervening* on hotspots — how they respond to targeted suppression. They showed that hotspots suppressed only locally can simply relocate ("displacement") rather than disappear, and characterized the conditions under which suppression instead causes a hotspot to dissipate. This paper is the closest existing analogue to the dissertation's core research question and its absence from the current draft is a significant gap: it is, in effect, the mathematical-modeling literature's own prior attempt to answer "what happens when you locally suppress attractiveness in part of a spatially heterogeneous landscape," which is exactly what introducing a greened zone into an otherwise-uniform lattice does in Whiteley's model. Engaging with it would let the dissertation state precisely how its ODE/ABM approach and findings (no significant displacement into buffer zones; benefits extending just past the greened boundary) agree with, extend, or diverge from Short, Bertozzi, and Brantingham's displacement/dissipation results.

### 3.3 Intervention-zone models: suppression, policing, and patch structure

A closely related line of work asks a version of the dissertation's own size/placement question, using a different intervention mechanism (active policing or a payoff-suppression zone rather than permanent greening). Berestycki, Rodriguez, and Ryzhik (2013) study a reaction-diffusion crime model on the real line and prove that a finite "gap" region where the payoff for crime is reduced to zero can permanently block an advancing crime wave only if the gap exceeds a critical length \(L^*\); below that length, the wave eventually overwhelms the gap. They further test splitting a fixed total gap length into two separated sub-intervals and find that splitting is never beneficial — the combined effect never outperforms a single unbroken interval of the same total length. This is a direct mathematical precedent for the dissertation's research questions 2 and 3, obtained for a different intervention mechanism, and the dissertation should explicitly state whether its own greening results agree with or diverge from this "splitting never helps" finding, since a divergence would itself be a notable result. Jones, Brantingham, and Chayes (2010) extend the original Short et al. lattice/PDE model with an explicit police-deployment field, showing how the shape and intensity of enforcement changes hotspot dissipation versus displacement, and Lloyd and O'Farrell (2013) characterize the localized spatial hotspot patterns (pulses, spots, and hexagon patches) that the same family of models can support. None of these three papers models a permanent land-use or environmental change of the kind greening represents, which is the dissertation's clearest point of methodological distinction from them.

Most directly relevant of all, Calatayud, Jornet, and Mateu (2025) extend crime compartmental models to spatially heterogeneous settings using exactly the dissertation's own pairing of tools: ordinary differential equations structured across discrete "patches" alongside a reaction-diffusion PDE formulation. Given the near-identical framing — a patch-structured ODE model plus a continuum spatial model of crime — this paper needs to be read in full and engaged with directly in the dissertation, not merely cited. The literature review should state explicitly, with specifics, how Whiteley's patches (representing greened versus non-greened urban lots, parameterized from empirical greening trials) and her paired ODE/agent-based comparison differ from Calatayud et al.'s patch structure and compartments. Until that comparison is made explicit, the dissertation's claim to originality in combining a patch-structured ODE model with a spatial crime model cannot be fully supported.

### 3.4 Compartmental (ODE) models of crime as contagion

Srivastav, Ghosh, and Chandra (2019) model crime using a susceptible-infected-recovered-style compartmental ODE system, sorting the population into noncriminal, criminal, incarcerated, and recovered classes with transition rates governed by policing and counseling. Their central result is a threshold ("basic reproduction number" R0) below which the system converges to a stable crime-free equilibrium and above which a stable positive-crime equilibrium exists instead — a direct mathematical analogue to epidemic threshold theory. This is the clearest existing precedent for the dissertation's own mean-field ODE patch model and is useful chiefly as a methodological comparison: Srivastav et al. model population compartments without spatial structure, whereas the dissertation's contribution is to add exactly that spatial structure (a greened patch versus a normal patch) to a similarly-motivated mean-field system.

### 3.5 Agent-based modeling methods in urban and health contexts

Auchincloss and Garcia (2015) provide a methodological introduction to agent-based modeling for urban-health researchers, covering how to decide what to represent as an agent, how to calibrate transition rules against real-world data, and common pitfalls in interpreting emergent model behavior. Although already in the dissertation's bibliography, this reference is never engaged with in the literature review text; it belongs squarely in a methods-justification section explaining *why* an ABM (rather than, say, a purely statistical regression model) is an appropriate tool for the greening question, complementing the mean-field ODE model with heterogeneity that ODEs cannot represent.

### 3.6 Principles of model verification and validation

Bender (2000) sets out a general four-step framework for mathematical modeling — define the question, decide what is in and out of scope, check the model is useful for the question at hand, and test whether the model's outputs behave as expected under known limiting cases — and argues that model validity should be assessed by checking these limiting cases explicitly. This is the correct citation for the dissertation's own model-verification chapter (e.g., checking the no-broken-windows/no-repeat-crime limiting case), and its placement in the literature review should point forward explicitly to where that verification is performed later in the dissertation.

## 4. Synthesis: Locating the Gap

Three literatures bear on this dissertation's questions, and none of them fully answers all three. Environmental criminology (Section 1) explains *why* greening should reduce crime — by removing a location from the crime triangle, adding guardianship and natural surveillance (defensible space, CPTED), and potentially activating collective efficacy — but these theories are almost entirely non-spatial and non-quantitative; they predict a direction of effect, not how effect size should scale with a greened area's size or configuration. The empirical greening literature (Section 2) is strong on causal identification — several randomized and quasi-experimental studies now agree that greening modestly but consistently reduces violent crime and fear of crime, without displacing it to nearby blocks — but these studies test specific, already-built interventions of fixed size and location; none experimentally varies patch size or the number/distribution of patches at fixed total area, because doing so in the field would be prohibitively expensive.

The mathematical crime-modeling literature (Section 3) is where the dissertation's actual competition lies, and here the gap claim requires more care than a first pass suggests. Short et al.'s (2008) lattice model and its PDE extension (Short, Bertozzi, & Brantingham, 2010) show that spatial heterogeneity in attractiveness produces nontrivial hotspot dynamics, including displacement, but treat the landscape as homogeneous rather than partitioned into distinct zones. Several papers do go further and study exactly the size/placement question the dissertation asks, using a different intervention mechanism than greening: Berestycki, Rodriguez, and Ryzhik (2013) derive a critical minimum length for a payoff-suppression zone to block a crime wave and show that splitting a fixed budget into two zones is never beneficial; Jones, Brantingham, and Chayes (2010) study how the shape and intensity of active police deployment changes hotspot outcomes. Most importantly, Calatayud, Jornet, and Mateu (2025) already combine a patch-structured ODE model with a spatially continuous crime model — the same pairing of tools this dissertation uses.

This means the dissertation's originality cannot rest on "no one has modeled spatial heterogeneity or intervention-zone geometry in a Short-type crime model," because several groups already have. The originality claim that the evidence *does* support is narrower and more specific: this dissertation is the first, to this reviewer's knowledge, to parameterize the intervention as a permanent environmental/land-use change calibrated against real greening-trial effect sizes (Branas et al., 2011, 2018; Garvin, Cannuscio, & Branas, 2013) rather than as an abstract payoff-suppression or active-policing term, and to use a paired ODE/agent-based comparison specifically to test how that empirically grounded intervention's size and spatial distribution affect outcomes. Whether this narrower claim holds depends on a direct, explicit comparison — in the dissertation text, not just in citation — against Berestycki, Rodriguez, and Ryzhik's (2013) "splitting never helps" result and against Calatayud, Jornet, and Mateu's (2025) patch-and-reaction-diffusion framework. That comparison is the single most important piece of unfinished work this review identifies: without it, the novelty section of the dissertation is asserting a gap rather than demonstrating one.

## Bridge

The dissertation uses two complementary models built around a shared "enticement" state variable (an environmental attractiveness-to-crime score that rises with recent crime, diffuses to nearby areas, and decays to a baseline). The first is a two-patch ODE compartmental model with a greened and a normal zone, each tracked by criminal density and enticement, plus a shared pool of "resting" (inactive/arrested) criminals; criminals migrate between patches at a rate driven by the ratio of destination-to-origin enticement, patch density, and shared-boundary length, while enticement in each patch rises with local crime, diffuses across the boundary proportional to the enticement gap, and decays toward a patch-specific baseline that is deliberately set lower for the greened zone. She verifies the model by taking the limiting case with no broken-windows effect and no near-repeat diffusion (θ = η = 0), proving via the standard ODE existence-uniqueness theorem that a stable equilibrium exists, and showing the greened/normal equilibria collapse to the same value when the zones are undifferentiated — a sensible sanity check. The second model is a NetLogo agent-based model on a lattice with four zone types (green, a bordering "buffer" zone, a same-size-and-shape "pink" control zone placed away from the green area, and a residual "black"/normal zone) — this pink-vs-buffer pairing is a nice design choice letting her isolate proximity effects from confounds. Together, the ODE model gives her a tractable, analytically verifiable two-zone system, while the ABM lets her test spatial questions the ODE can't — number/shape of greened patches, spillover into buffer zones — using the same enticement mechanics, which is exactly the kind of paired-model design the newly added Calatayud et al. (2025) and Berestycki et al. (2013) papers should be checked against for overlap.

Community-driven disorder reduction: Crime prevention through a clean and green initiative in a legacy city. *Urban Studies, 57*(14), 2956–2972. *Studies Flint, MI's Clean and Green program (2005–2014); finds greening and active maintenance associated with emergent crime "cold spots" and improved resident perceptions of safety.*

**Sampson, R. J., Raudenbush, S. W., & Earls, F.** (1997). Neighborhoods and violent crime: A multilevel study of collective efficacy. *Science, 277*(5328), 918–924. [Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252316/). *Multilevel study of Chicago neighborhoods finding collective efficacy — social cohesion plus willingness to intervene — more predictive of reduced violence than poverty or instability alone; offers a socioeconomic-heterogeneity mechanism complementary to the physical-design literature.*

**Short, M. B., D'Orsogna, M. R., Pasour, V. B., Tita, G. E., Brantingham, P. J., Bertozzi, A. L., & Chayes, L. B.** (2008). A statistical model of criminal behavior. *Mathematical Models and Methods in Applied Sciences, 18*(supp01), 1249–1267. *The foundational lattice/agent-based attractiveness model this dissertation directly extends; shows crime hotspots can emerge endogenously from local diffusion and decay rules alone.*

**Short, M. B., Bertozzi, A. L., & Brantingham, P. J.** (2010). Dissipation and displacement of hotspots in reaction-diffusion models of crime. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 107*(9), 3961–3965. [Link](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2010/short2010a.pdf). *PDE continuum limit of Short et al. (2008); characterizes when suppressing a hotspot causes it to dissipate versus merely relocate — the closest existing mathematical precedent to the dissertation's own greened-patch questions and a significant omission from the current draft.*

**South, E. C., Hohl, B. C., Kondo, M. C., MacDonald, J. M., & Branas, C. C.** (2018). Effect of greening vacant land on mental health of community-dwelling adults: A cluster randomized trial. *JAMA Network Open, 1*(3), e180298. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.0298). *Using the same Philadelphia RCT as Branas et al. (2018), finds greening significantly reduced self-reported depression and worthlessness, with the largest effects among residents below the poverty line.*

**Srivastav, A. K., Ghosh, M., & Chandra, P.** (2019). Modeling dynamics of the spread of crime in a society. *Stochastic Analysis and Applications, 37*(6), 991–1011. *SIR-style compartmental ODE model of crime as contagion; establishes a reproduction-number threshold between crime-free and endemic-crime equilibria, the closest existing precedent for the dissertation's own mean-field patch model.*

**Townsley, M., Homel, R., & Chaseling, J.** (2003). Infectious burglaries: A test of the near repeat hypothesis. *British Journal of Criminology, 43*(3), 615–633. *Confirms significant space-time clustering of burglary that decays over roughly two months, stronger in neighborhoods with homogeneous housing stock; direct empirical motivation for the model's spatial diffusion term.*

**U.S. Department of Justice, Office of Justice Programs, Community Capacity Development Office.** (2004). *Weed and Seed strategy*. *Describes the federal program pairing law-enforcement "weeding" with community-development "seeding"; useful contrast case since it combines greening-adjacent investment with active policing.*

**Wilson, J. Q., & Kelling, G. L.** (1982). Broken windows. *Atlantic Monthly, 249*(3), 29–38. *Originates the broken-windows thesis that visible disorder invites further crime; the mechanism the dissertation's dynamic-enticement term formalizes, though see Harcourt (2001) for the major empirical critique.*
