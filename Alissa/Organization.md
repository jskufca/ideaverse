# A Few Thoughts on Dissertation Organization

*Some things to chew on before we talk — not a checklist, just food for thought.*

Every dissertation eventually runs into the same question: "I know what I want to say, but where does this piece actually go?" That's not really a formatting question — it's a question about what job each section of a dissertation is supposed to do. Once you're clear on the *job*, the *placement* usually sorts itself out.

Two places worth having open on your desk while you think about this:

- **Nicholas Higham's *Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences*** (SIAM, 3rd ed.) has a chapter specifically on writing a thesis, plus a companion chapter on writing a paper that covers structure, the role of the introduction, and how a conclusion should function. It's written for exactly your field (applied math), so its advice tends to map onto our conventions more cleanly than generic writing guides.
- **ANSI/NISO Z39.18**, *Scientific and Technical Reports — Preparation, Presentation, and Preservation*, is a formal standard (not math-specific) that lays out a generic skeleton every technical document tends to converge on: front matter, then a body running Introduction → Methods → Results and Discussion → Conclusions → Recommendations → References, then back matter with Appendices. It's a useful sanity check — if your dissertation's structure looks wildly different from this skeleton, that's worth asking yourself why.

Here are a few distinctions that are easy to blur, but which — once you see them clearly — tend to make the whole document easier to organize.

**Background vs. Literature Review.** These get lumped into one chapter all the time, but they're doing different work. *Background* is the stuff a reader must understand before your own contribution makes sense — notation, prior definitions, theorems or models you're building directly on. *Literature Review* is different: it's making an argument about where the field currently stands, what's settled, what's contested, and — crucially — where the open space is that your work occupies. A background section explains; a literature review argues a position. Worth asking of each piece of "review" writing: am I helping the reader understand something, or am I making a case about where my work fits?

**Discussion vs. Conclusion.** These often collapse into "restate the results, twice." A cleaner way to think about it: your *Discussion* should stay inside the fence of what you've actually documented — your results, interpreted using the theory and prior work you've already built up earlier in the document. Your *Conclusion* is where you're allowed to step outside that fence a little — bring in things you haven't necessarily built a formal case for elsewhere, gesture at broader implications, or even venture an intuition you can't fully prove. If your discussion sections and your conclusion currently read almost the same, that's usually a sign the conclusion isn't doing its own job yet.

**Appendix vs. Background.** Both feel like "extra material," but they serve opposite purposes. Background is required reading — you can't follow the main argument without it. An appendix is the reverse: material a reader could skip entirely and still follow your argument, but which is genuinely useful to have written down somewhere — a longer derivation, a full parameter table, verification details that would otherwise slow down the narrative. A good test: if you deleted this section, would a reader get lost, or would they just miss out on something nice? Lost → background (or main body). Miss out → appendix. Most dissertations under-use appendices; it's a good tool to reach for more often than students think.

None of this is about rewriting anything right now — it's just a lens. As you read back through what you've got, try asking each section "what job is this actually doing?" and see if the answer surprises you anywhere.
