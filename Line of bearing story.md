It was a dark and stormy night.   Well, actually ... it was very early morning, and the storm had passed.   Our submarine was returning to kings Bay georgia at the end of deployment and we are on the last open water leg before making the turn into the channel.   I was the navigator, and called up to the bridge and said "mark the turn" which tells them that i think its time to start turning the ship.   But they don't turn.  My team is continuing to report and a few seconds pass and I tell the captain that we are 100 yard past the turn and he tells me that they pilot has good visibility and is holding the turn a bit longer.   

And then, after a short pause, I say the line that I am never supposed to say "the navigator does not concur.  Recommend full rudder turn now."   My Assistant navigator immediately turned to the log keeper and told him to "enter that in the logs!"   The control room fell completely quiet and then, thankfully, within a couple of seconds, the order from the bridge ... "left full rudder, steady course 266."


I need to help you appreciate the challenge here.   Our ship was a Trident class submarine, as long as a couple of football fields end to end, with a small rudder for our size on one of the largest ships in the Navy.   trying to pull into a channel ... that is narrower than the length of our ship.   When the rudder goes overs, there are still a few seconds before the ship even begins to turn.  My navigation team continued to track us through the turn.  At some point, the fathometer gave us a warning that were at the far edge of the channel, with just a couple of feet of water beneath the keel.   But by then the ship was finally completing is turn.   We were on the far right edge of the channel, but we never touched bottom.


There are actually many lines in this story.  One of those would be the line-of-bearing to the channel range lights ... which ... if you are on the centerline of the channel line up exactly at a bearing of 268 ... channel course.   They serve as the key guide visual guide.   But those range lights are about 10 miles away which means ... if your on the extreme edge of the channel, the mismatch in those range lights is less than one arc second.   You all know ... and arcsecond.   Yeah ... 360 degrees in a circle.  Take one of those degrees, split it into 60 pieces, thats an arcsecond, which is about at the limits of the human eye.   There is very little margin for error.

The other line is the line I'm supposed to say: "Navigator concurs".   You see, when pulling into port on a submarine, those folks up on the bridge (the captain, supported by a pilot ... an expert on driving ships into port) have the best field of view and awareness, but the navigation team below is using all kinds of information form sonar, the periscopes, fathometer, etc ... as backup.
